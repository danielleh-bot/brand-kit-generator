Inside the boardroom shake-up that is quietly reshaping America's biggest tech companies

A handful of activist investors are pushing for sharper governance, leaner org charts, and a return to founder-mode decision making — and Silicon Valley is paying attention.

By Madeline Berg and Reed Albergotti Updated Jun. 8, 2026, 6:30 AM ET

When the proxy fight at one of Silicon Valley's biggest names quietly settled last month — without a press conference, without a public shareholder vote, and without a single tweet from the CEO — it marked something that observers say has been building for nearly two years: a generational shift in how America's most valuable technology companies are governed.

Interviews with 14 current and former directors, four activist fund partners, and a half-dozen advisers reveal a coordinated push to flatten executive teams, reinstate dual-class founder voting at companies where it was watered down in the 2010s, and tie a larger share of CEO compensation to medium-term operating metrics rather than stock price.

"It's not that the founders were always right," said one director who has served on three FAANG-adjacent boards. "It's that the committees got so layered that nobody could remember who had decision rights anymore. Activists are arbitraging that confusion."

The shift is showing up in compensation filings, too. Median CEO pay across the seven largest US tech companies fell 11% last year, while operating-metric-linked equity rose to its highest share in a decade. Several boards have introduced "founder veto" clauses on org-design decisions — a structure unheard of even three years ago.

Business Insider has reviewed internal memos from two of the companies described in this story. Both declined to comment on the record.
